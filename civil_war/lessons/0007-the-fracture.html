---the-fracture--- Lesson 7: The Fracture (1856–1861)

Season 1, Episode 7 — The Fracture, 1856–1861

The Fracture

In the late 1850s, the line of fracture ran straight across the small world of West Point. Cadets who had been roommates for years started choosing sides. Some stopped speaking. Some came to blows.

The Changing Mood at West Point

In the early 1850s, J.E.B. Stuart (then a cadet) wrote that there was "no North or South" among the cadets. By 1859, that was a lie. The corps of cadets was split "into two parties, hostile in sentiment and even divided in barracks." The 1850s saw the Compromise of 1850, the Kansas-Nebraska Act, the Dred Scott decision — each one drove the wedge deeper.

The Emory Upton Brawl

Emory Upton (Class of 1861) was an avowed abolitionist from New York. Wade Hampton Gibbes (Class of 1861) was a South Carolina cadet. Gibbes accused Upton of "intimate association with negroes" because Upton had attended Oberlin College — the first US college to admit Black students. Upton was smaller than Gibbes but challenged him anyway. The fight was brutal — Upton got a deep cut on his face from a bayonet but held his own. Southern cadets hurled abuse at Upton throughout the melee. This was the moment the mask came off: cadets were willing to bleed for their beliefs.

George Thomas — "The Virginian Who Betrayed Virginia"

George Thomas was a Virginia-born Union general. He was the "Rock of Chickamauga" — Grant's most reliable commander in the West. His decision to stay with the Union was agonizing. His family disowned him. His sisters turned his portrait to the wall and never spoke to him again. He was a West Point instructor before the war, teaching cavalry tactics. One of his students was J.E.B. Stuart (Confederate cavalry commander). Another was John Bell Hood (Confederate general who later fought against Thomas at Franklin and Nashville). Thomas beat Hood so thoroughly at Nashville (December 1864) that Hood's army ceased to exist. His own family in Virginia considered him a traitor until the day they died.

Beauregard's 5-Day Superintendency

P.G.T. Beauregard (Class of 1838) was appointed Superintendent of West Point on January 23, 1861. He served for exactly 5 days. He was removed because his views were too pro-secession. It is the shortest superintendency in West Point history. Beauregard then went to Charleston — and 3 months later fired the first shot of the Civil War at Fort Sumter.

Custer at West Point

George Armstrong Custer was in the Class of 1861. He graduated LAST (34th of 34). He was almost expelled — he failed to break up a cadet fight (ironic, given the Upton brawl). He accumulated near-record demerits — 726 in his first year alone. But he was popular, charismatic, and a brilliant horseman (like Grant). He was the only member of his class to become a general.

The Classes of 1861

The War Department experimented with a 5-year program — so the class of 1856 graduated in May 1861, and the class of 1857 graduated in June 1861. TWO graduations in one tragic spring. Of the June class: 23 men left when their states seceded; 34 graduated — of whom 4 immediately resigned to "go south." These men went straight from graduation to the battlefield.

The Edmund Kirby Story

Edmund Kirby (Class of 1861) was a quiet, religious cadet — a leader in prayer meetings. He stayed with the Union. At First Bull Run (July 1861), he was shot in the leg. The contract surgeon bungled the wound. Infection set in. The leg was amputated — but it was too late. As he lay dying in a Washington hospital, Abraham Lincoln visited him. Lincoln made Kirby a brigadier general (a brevet promotion) as a deathbed gift — ensuring his family would get a pension. Kirby smiled, thanked the president, and died. His classmates in both armies grieved when they heard.

  • George Thomas: Virginian who stayed Union. Family disowned him.
  • Custer: Class of 1861, last in his class, almost expelled
  • Emory Upton brawl: abolitionist vs. pro-slavery cadets at West Point
  • Two graduations in 1861: May and June — both classes sent straight to war
