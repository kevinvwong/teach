Section 2: Odd One Out

  1. d) Grant — The other three are all Class of 1842; Grant is Class of 1843.
  2. d) Class of 1765 — West Point was founded in 1802; the other three are real classes that produced dozens of Civil War generals.
  3. a) McClellan — The only Union general of the four; Jackson, Pickett, and Longstreet all fought for the Confederacy.
  4. c) D.H. Hill — The only Confederate of the four; Rosecrans, Pope, and Custer were Union generals.
  5. d) Lee — Grant, Longstreet, and Sherman are all woven together through Grant's web of friendships and West Point connections; Lee stands apart from that specific network.

Section 3: Who Am I?

  1. Ulysses S. Grant (born Hiram Ulysses Grant; the "S." came from a congressional appointment paperwork error)
  2. Robert E. Lee (second in the Class of 1829, no demerits, married Mary Custis of Arlington)
  3. Stonewall Jackson (Class of 1846, ranked 17th; his nickname may have begun as "stand like a stone wall" — an insult or compliment depending on who you ask)
  4. James Longstreet (ranked 54th of 56; best man at Grant's 1848 wedding; opposed Pickett's Charge; became a Reconstruction Republican)
  5. William Rosecrans (ranked 5th in the Class of 1842; outranked Grant early in the war; defeated at Chickamauga by Longstreet in 1863)
  6. John Pope (ranked 17th in the Class of 1842; took command of the Army of Virginia boasting; crushed by Lee and Jackson at Second Bull Run)
  7. George Pickett (dead last in the Class of 1846; Longstreet commanded the overall assault on July 3, 1863 — Pickett led only his division)
  8. Jefferson Davis (Class of 1828; U.S. Secretary of War under Pierce; President of the Confederate States)
  9. Simon Bolivar Buckner (best man at Grant's wedding; surrendered Fort Donelson to Grant; marched in Grant's 1885 funeral)
  10. Winfield Scott Hancock (Union general at Gettysburg; his close friend Lewis Armistead led a Confederate brigade in Pickett's Charge and died calling for him)

Section 4: Story Connections

  1. Love triangle. A.P. Hill and George McClellan both courted Ellen Marcy. She chose McClellan, which poisoned Hill's relationship with his future commanding general and may have affected Hill's performance in the Seven Days Battles.
  2. Post-war politics. Longstreet became a Republican after the war — Grant's party. This destroyed Longstreet's reputation in the South and strained his relationship with former Confederate comrades. Grant appointed him to federal positions as a reward, but Southerners never forgave him.
  3. They produced the war's key commanders. The Class of 1842 gave the war Longstreet, D.H. Hill, Rosecrans, and Pope. The Class of 1843 gave the war Grant. The Class of 1846 gave the war Jackson, McClellan, Pickett, and 22 generals total. Their cadets overlapped at West Point, creating the personal web that defined command relationships on both sides.
  4. Marriage and academia. D.H. Hill married Stonewall Jackson's sister Isabella, making them brothers-in-law. After the war, Hill became a college president (University of Arkansas, then Alabama A&M — now Auburn); Jackson was a VMI professor before the war. Both men blended military careers with academic life.
  5. The training ground for Civil War commanders. Nearly every major Civil War general — Grant, Lee, Jackson, Longstreet, McClellan, Sherman — served in the Mexican-American War (1846–1848). They observed the same battles, learned the same lessons about leadership and logistics, and formed the relationships (friendships and feuds) that defined the Civil War. Appomattox was the closing act of a story that began in Mexico.

Section 5: True or False

  1. False. Longstreet commanded the overall assault; Pickett led only his division. The charge is named for Pickett's division, not the tactical commander.
  2. False. His real name was Hiram Ulysses Grant. The "S." was added by a clerical error during his congressional appointment to West Point.
  3. True. Lee graduated second in his class with a perfect disciplinary record.
  4. True. The Class of 1846 produced 22 generals — more than any other West Point class in history.
  5. False. Longstreet strongly opposed the frontal assault on the Union center at Cemetery Ridge. He predicted it would fail, and he was right.
